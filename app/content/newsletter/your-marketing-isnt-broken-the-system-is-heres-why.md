If you doubled your marketing spend tomorrow, could you double your sales?

Most cannabis marketing does not fail because teams spend too little. It fails because marketing is asked to manufacture success while known problems remain unresolved.

I asked John Shute why marketing is the easiest thing to cut, and how teams can diagnose what is actually broken before touching budget.

Marketing does not create value. It distributes value that already exists. When distribution underperforms, the instinct is to blame the channel. The reality is almost always structural.

Modern tracking makes attribution clearer and cleaner than ever. Attribution is simply knowing which actions produce results and which do not. Traffic without conversion is opportunity. Opportunity is a diagnostic signal.

The fastest way to uncover the truth is to switch sides. Put yourself in the customer's shoes. Walk the store. Click your own links. Try to find your product. Try to buy it. Try to get help. Notice what feels slow, confusing, awkward, or out of place. Every moment of friction is a tax on your marketing spend.

This is work anyone can do today. No tools required. No budget approval. Just attention.

If messaging is unclear, if the in store experience does not match the online promise, or if follow up is inconsistent, those are not reasons to reduce spend. Those are the exact areas that need to be fortified before spending more.

These fixes are defendable. They compound. They determine whether marketing dollars amplify or evaporate.

This is why marketing feels unpredictable. It explains why some teams struggle even with massive budgets, while others explode with very little spend. The difference is not talent or tactics. It is whether the system can absorb demand.

Most leaders believe attribution is harder than it is. In reality, it is ignored more than it is unavailable. When teams do not understand what is working, confusion sets in. Marketing gets cut as a reaction to uncertainty, not as evidence of failure.

Marketing works when it operates as a closed loop system, not a collection of disconnected tactics.

Before cutting your budget, ask a sharper question. If we doubled our spend, would the return scale, or would it simply expose what is already broken.

This is not a marketing problem. It is a systems problem.
