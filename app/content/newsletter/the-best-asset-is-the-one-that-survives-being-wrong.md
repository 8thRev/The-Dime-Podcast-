**The companies asking for reform do not all benefit from the same change.**

A large MSO with access to capital, multiple state licenses, compliance teams, and exposure to hemp may want something completely different from a smaller operator, a processor, or a flower brand.

The same rule can help one and hurt another.

**The obstacle and the moat can be the same thing.**

Reform starts changing those barriers. Once they fall, new competitors enter the market, and the advantage shifts again.

We talk about reform as if removing friction improves the market for everyone.

It does not.

A new compliance standard can create a cost one company cannot absorb while making another company’s existing infrastructure more valuable.

Too few retailers can leave a flower company sitting on inventory while strengthening a processor.

Federal limbo can frustrate a large operator while also protecting it from smaller competitors that cannot afford the same legal, financial, and regulatory complexity.

Canada offers one of the clearest examples.

Years ago, cannabis companies built around the expectation that Canadian operators would eventually serve a much larger global market.

Much of that future did not arrive the way they expected.

Capital disappeared. Strategies changed. Assets that once represented aggressive expansion started looking like excess.

But some operators had also built regulated medical infrastructure, international distribution, quality systems, and facilities designed for markets with higher operating standards.

The original forecast was wrong.

Some of the capabilities were still right.

**That is the part operators should pay attention to.**

Being early only matters if what you built still has value when the thesis changes.

Most companies try to predict the outcome.

What will Washington do?

When will it happen?

What changes with banking, interstate commerce, hemp, or rescheduling?

Those questions matter.

But every prediction adds another assumption to the business.

A better question is:

**What can we build today that remains valuable if our prediction is wrong?**

- Quality systems
- Distribution knowledge
- Facilities built to higher standards
- Processes that make additional compliance easier to absorb
- Infrastructure that would take the next operator years and significant capital to recreate

Those capabilities give the business more than one way to win.

A business built for one narrow future works if prices hold, licenses remain scarce, capital opens up, regulation moves on schedule, and new entrants behave the way the model expects.

They rarely do.

That is the difference between an expensive asset and real optionality.

An expensive asset needs the original thesis to be right.

**Optionality keeps creating value after the thesis changes.**

The company protected by regulation yesterday may tomorrow be competing against operators with cheaper capital, deeper infrastructure, stronger brands, or years of experience outside cannabis.

The old moat can disappear.

The capabilities underneath it do not have to.

Which assets become more valuable as standards rise?

Which systems absorb complexity faster than a competitor?

What is difficult to recreate?

What would another operator rather buy than spend years building?

Those are more useful questions than trying to perfectly predict the next rule.

**The operator’s job is to build a company that can survive more than one version of the future.**

The strongest companies may not predict that future correctly.

They may simply build something valuable enough to survive being wrong.

**The best asset is not the one built for the future everyone expects.**

**It is the one that still matters when that future fails.**
