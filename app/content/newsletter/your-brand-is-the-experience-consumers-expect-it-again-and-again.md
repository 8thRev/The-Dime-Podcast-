Before someone tries your brand, they’re already imagining how it will make them feel.

That matters in cannabis because the first purchase requires trust before the experience has even happened.

The first purchase carries that anticipation.

The second carries an expectation.

They’ve felt it. Now they want it again.

They remember how it tasted, how long it took to feel something, and whether the experience fit the evening they wanted.

For marketing teams, think about all those dollars spent refining copy.

Debating the design.

Getting the packaging just right.

Your packaging helps give someone the courage to choose your product among all the options.

Your operation has to deliver the experience.

And the stakes can be bigger than losing one future sale.

For someone who is still cannabis-curious, this experience can determine whether they come back or write off the entire category.

In my conversation with Kim Sanchez Rael of Azuca, we kept coming back to a simple question:

What makes someone trust a product enough to buy it again?

The consumer may never understand the infusion technology. But they know what happened last time. And your name is on the package they’re buying again.

That experience is influenced by decisions they will never see.

The ingredient.

The manufacturing process.

How the product holds up before someone opens it.

What works in a sample still has to survive cooking, curing, and time in the package.

Each step can affect what the consumer experiences.

A gummy that sweats in the package, takes longer than expected to hit, tastes different, or simply feels different from the last one may look like a consumer problem at the end of the chain.

But the cause can start much earlier.

That is why supplier selection is part of the brand experience too.

Kim described a manufacturer whose infusion supplier could not meet its production schedule for roughly six weeks. The team had already started qualifying Azuca’s process.

She loaded ingredients and equipment into her truck and delivered them herself so production could continue.

The consumer never sees that moment.

They just see whether the product they expected is there when they want to buy it.

Supplier decisions, production decisions, and quality checks all contribute to those answers.

All the work that went into earning that second purchase now depends on what came out of the package.

This is one of the ideas that has shaped how I think about Newton.

If you want to repeat an outcome, you need to understand the process that created it.

What happened during the run?

What changed from the last one?

Can the team see the difference and understand why it matters?

Customer feedback can tell you the experience changed. Process visibility gives you somewhere to start figuring out why.

The customer should not have to be the first person who tells you something is different.

Of course, the same product will not affect every person identically. Kim pointed to metabolism, meals, and tolerance. Those differences exist.

That makes the variables the business can influence even more important.

A brand promise starts outside the facility.

The consumer sees the package. Reads the claims. Hears about the product from a friend. Remembers the last time they bought it.

But fulfilling that promise happens inside the operation.

It happens through the ingredient choices, the suppliers, the manufacturing process, the quality checks, and the ability to understand when something changes.

The first purchase carries anticipation.

The experience creates the expectation.

Every purchase after that asks you to deliver it again.

Marketing spends money building that expectation. Every batch puts it to the test.
