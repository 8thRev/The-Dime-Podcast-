Cannabis can be legal, available, and increasingly visible while people remain uncomfortable discussing it publicly. Ethan Zohn discovered that firsthand while interviewing World Cup fans.

**For operators, legalization creates access. Normalization builds the comfort and confidence that help people participate in the market.** Those are two very different challenges.

The cannabis industry has a habit of treating legalization as the finish line.

A state opens a market. Dispensaries begin selling. Operators focus on licenses, pricing, market share, and customer acquisition.

All important metrics. But none of them tell us whether people are actually comfortable with cannabis.

**Legalization changes what people are allowed to do. It doesn't automatically change how they feel about doing it.**

## What Ethan Zohn Discovered at the World Cup

Ethan Zohn, winner of *Survivor: Africa*, encountered that contradiction while filming *Kicking Back*, a soccer and cannabis-themed World Cup travel series. He expected cannabis to be more prevalent and people to be more willing to discuss it.

Instead, he encountered something that surprised him.

Fans were openly drinking, celebrating, and pouring beers over their heads. But when Ethan brought up cannabis, some of those same people became uncomfortable.

At one point, Ethan estimated that for every interview viewers saw, he may have approached five to ten people who didn't want to discuss cannabis on camera.

Think about that contrast.

Thousands of people celebrating one of the world's largest sporting events, comfortable displaying their relationship with alcohol, yet hesitant to publicly discuss cannabis.

**Cannabis had become legally accessible in many of the places Ethan visited, but that didn't mean it had become socially acceptable.**

And the hesitation wasn't universal. Ethan observed that younger fans generally seemed more comfortable discussing cannabis publicly. Some international fans were surprised that they could legally walk into a dispensary in the United States.

Different backgrounds. Different perceptions. Different levels of comfort.

All participating in the same broader market.

## The Industry's Normalization Problem

Inside the cannabis industry, we live in a bubble.

Our colleagues talk about cannabis. Our friends consume it. We attend events where discussing products, strains, and experiences is completely ordinary.

We can easily forget that this isn't how the rest of the world operates.

Ethan recognized that bias in himself. Soccer and cannabis naturally went together in his social circle. He expected to encounter that same familiarity while traveling.

He didn't.

**We risk confusing the comfort of the cannabis community with the acceptance of the broader consumer market.**

Consider the customer who's interested in trying cannabis but doesn't know where to start.

Are they comfortable walking into a dispensary? Asking a basic question? Being seen leaving with a purchase?

Or do they still associate cannabis with something they need to keep private?

These are not necessarily objections to the product itself. They're barriers to participation.

And they're difficult to identify by looking at sales data alone.

## What This Means for Operators

For years, the industry has focused on expanding access. More states, more stores, more products, and more opportunities for consumers to purchase cannabis legally.

But access is only part of the equation.

**A customer can have access to cannabis and still lack the confidence to participate in the market.**

That creates a different set of challenges for operators.

A dispensary built around experienced consumers may unintentionally intimidate someone entering for the first time.

Marketing that assumes everyone understands cannabis terminology can reinforce the feeling that a new consumer doesn't belong.

And a brand that focuses exclusively on existing cannabis enthusiasts may overlook the much larger audience that remains curious but hesitant.

This is where education, approachable experiences, and community engagement become meaningful.

Ethan offered an example outside the traditional retail environment. He organized a pickup soccer event in New York City that brought people together through a shared interest in sports, with cannabis as part of the surrounding community experience.

The soccer provided a familiar entry point. Cannabis didn't have to be the first conversation.

That distinction matters.

Not every customer needs to be persuaded to consume cannabis. Some simply need an environment where they feel comfortable asking questions, learning, or deciding whether it is right for them.

For patients, those conversations carry additional importance. Ethan's experience seeking cannabis guidance during cancer treatment highlights the need for qualified medical oversight and honest discussions about risks, potential benefits, and interactions with other treatments.

A legal storefront cannot replace that guidance.

## The Next Stage of Cannabis Normalization

None of this suggests operators can eliminate stigma on their own.

Laws, culture, generational attitudes, and personal experiences all influence how people perceive cannabis.

And someone declining an interview on camera doesn't necessarily mean they're opposed to cannabis. Privacy and personal preference matter.

But Ethan's experience exposes something the industry should pay closer attention to.

We've spent years measuring the growth of legal cannabis through the number of markets opening, stores operating, and products being sold.

Those measurements tell us how the legal industry is expanding.

They tell us far less about how many people still hesitate to engage with it.

**Legalization creates the opportunity for a transaction. Normalization creates the confidence to participate.**

The industry's next stage of growth depends on understanding the difference.
