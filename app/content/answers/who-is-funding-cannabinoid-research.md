For most of cannabinoid science's history, the money only flowed one direction: toward proving harm. Dr. Marcel Bonn-Miller, who has done cannabinoid research for about 23 years, said that in the early days nearly everyone in the field studied harms, because that's where the funding was, and nobody was funding therapeutic research. That has changed, but not evenly, and not completely, as he and Joel Stanley discussed on [Cannabis Was Medicine. Now the Proof Is Catching Up](/episodes/cannabis-was-medicine-now-the-proof-is-catching-up-ft-joel-stanley-and-dr-marcel-bonn-miller).

## The funding base has diversified

Bonn-Miller said there's now a lot more funding coming from organizations beyond the National Institute on Drug Abuse, which historically only funded studies on cannabis-related harms. Complementary and alternative medicine divisions are now funding cannabinoid therapeutics research, including a growing focus on patients 65 and older, with the University of Colorado Boulder publishing studies in that area. Universities have built out real infrastructure around this too: multiple academic journals dedicated to cannabis research now exist, along with several conferences beyond the International Cannabinoid Research Society, and accredited degree and certificate programs, including one at Thomas Jefferson University that Bonn-Miller helped build the curriculum for.

## Corporate capital is entering through drug development, not consumer brands

The clearest sign of where serious money is going is Ozna Biosciences' lead drug candidate, AJA-001, a full-spectrum CBD/THC formulation targeting irritability and aggression in autism spectrum disorder. Joel Stanley, founder of Ozna and formerly founding CEO of Charlotte's Web, said the drug is partly funded by a $15 million investment from British American Tobacco through a partnership called Deloria. That's tobacco capital underwriting FDA-grade botanical drug development, not a wellness product line. It's a different funding logic than the consumer cannabinoid market, where companies like BayMedica fund their own biosynthesis and chemical synthesis R&D to produce minor cannabinoids such as CBC and THCV at commercial scale, as Shane Johnson described on [How Minor Cannabinoids are Made](/episodes/how-minor-cannabinoids-are-made-synthetic-vs-biosynthetic-ft-shane-johnson).

## The field is still understaffed relative to the work in front of it

Bonn-Miller said researchers who used to study alcohol are now shifting into cannabis, which is a good sign, but he also said there's still a mountain to climb and the field needs more people doing the work, because each individual cannabinoid represents years of research on its own. That's the real constraint right now. It isn't that nobody will fund cannabinoid science anymore. It's that the number of qualified people and standardized processes hasn't caught up to the number of compounds worth studying.

For an operator thinking about where to put research dollars, the lesson is to go where the funding infrastructure already exists rather than trying to build it from scratch. Partnering with university programs, publishing through the dedicated journals that now exist, and courting therapeutics-focused capital (not just consumer brand investors) are all more viable paths now than they were even a decade ago. But don't expect speed. Bonn-Miller's point stands: each cannabinoid is its own multi-year research project, and there simply aren't enough hands on deck yet.
