Cannabis payment processing companies get discussed as a retail checkout problem. That's too narrow. The instability at the dispensary counter pushes problems upstream into how businesses pay each other, and that's where the real money is bleeding out.

## Card networks treat cannabis as a federal legality proxy

Aubrey Amatelli, founder of PayRio, built what she thought was a bulletproof solution out of JP Morgan: follow Visa and Mastercard's rules, use the correct merchant category code, structure the funds flow properly. It didn't matter. As she put it, ["Visa, Mastercard, billion-dollar companies, they can come in and squash whatever they want to squash, even if we can fight and say, 'But look, we're doing it the right way.'"](/episodes/the-legal-market-was-never-built-for-payments-why-cannabis-still-breaks-the-rules-ft-aubrey-amatelli) Mastercard paused her company's processing to review how funds were flowing, and by the time of that conversation had already shut down one of the industry's larger processors, leaving over 5,000 dispensaries without payments for weeks. When a network pulls support, the bank sponsor has to shut the rail off immediately, and merchants can see funds frozen for up to 180 days.

That instability is not an inconvenience. It's a structural reason cannabis businesses stay cash-heavy and informal with each other, not just with consumers.

## Cash and unstable rails push businesses into acting like unregulated banks

Brett Gelfand, founder of Cannabis Collects and the Cannabiz Credit Association, has spent years chasing unpaid invoices across the industry. In [his conversation on The Dime](/episodes/if-youre-chasing-invoices-in-cannabis-listen-to-this-ft-brett-gelfand), he laid out why this happens: federal banking restrictions limit access to normal credit tools, so cannabis companies extend net-30 or net-60 terms to each other with no underwriting and no formal contracts. "When you extend terms, you're acting like a bank," Gelfand said, and most companies don't realize it until they're getting hammered with past-due money. A Whitney Economics study he cited found $3.8 billion in delinquent cannabis payments in 2023, more than half of it over 60 days past due. Gelfand's blunt framing: "You're basically an unsecured creditor. And the next thing you know, most of those unsecured creditors don't get paid."

## The two problems share one root cause

Payment processing instability and invoice delinquency both trace back to the same fact: cannabis sits outside the normal financial system that every other industry takes for granted. Amatelli's view is that rescheduling alone won't fix the payments side, because Visa and Mastercard require full federal legalization before they'll treat cannabis like any other retail category. Gelfand's data suggests the informal-credit problem won't go away on its own either, since it's a direct byproduct of the same banking restrictions.

For an operator, the practical takeaway is to stop treating these as separate departments. If you're extending terms to other cannabis businesses the way you'd extend a handshake, you're running an unsecured lending operation without realizing it. Gelfand's advice was to have an actual credit policy, check a buyer's payment history before extending terms, and escalate past-due accounts fast rather than waiting it out, since collection odds drop sharply after 90 days. On the processing side, Amatelli's advice was redundancy: never have all your payment rails running through one processor, because a Mastercard shutoff hits with no warning and no appeal. Both problems demand the same discipline that federal legality would otherwise hand you for free.
