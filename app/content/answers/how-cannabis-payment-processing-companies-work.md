A cannabis payment processing company is not, in most cases, a technology company. It's a sourcing and redundancy operation. That distinction matters more than the marketing language around "compliant" or "bulletproof" solutions suggests.

## They buy tech, they don't build it

Aubrey Amatelli, founder of PayRio, said it plainly in [The Legal Market Was Never Built for Payments](/episodes/the-legal-market-was-never-built-for-payments-why-cannabis-still-breaks-the-rules-ft-aubrey-amatelli): "Internally, PayRio doesn't build any products. I wish I had that developer mind, but what I do is source and find great products for the market, then partner with those companies to develop them uniquely for cannabis and bring them to market." That means when you sign with a cannabis payment processor, you're really buying access to a network of third-party tech providers that the processor has vetted, customized, and wrapped in cannabis-specific compliance work.

That sourcing model carries real financial risk for the processor itself. Amatelli put it this way: "Even though I'm not building the tech myself, I'm investing a lot of resources into these partnerships, and if it doesn't work out, you could be out six figures or more." If you're evaluating a processor, that's worth asking about directly: how many of their partnerships have failed, and what happened to the merchants on the other end of those failures.

## Redundancy is the actual product

The core value a cannabis payment processor sells isn't a card reader. It's survivability. Amatelli described building "multiple rails in one terminal, so if one gets shut down, another automatically turns on." This exists because Visa and Mastercard can and do pull support without an appeals process, freezing funds at the bank level for up to 180 days while a merchant scrambles. Amatelli watched Mastercard shut down one of the larger processors in the industry, leaving over 5,000 dispensaries without payments for weeks.

A processor that only has one rail is a processor that can go dark with no warning. Redundancy isn't a feature add-on. It's the entire reason these companies exist in a form different from a standard merchant services provider.

## They won't give you everything a normal retailer gets

Amatelli was direct about the limits: no Apple Pay, no Google Pay, no Amex, because "we won't bring something to market that falsifies an MCC code or risks a client being fined by Visa or Mastercard, or placed on a MATCH list, which bars a merchant from processing payments for six years." A processor willing to cut those corners is taking on risk it will eventually pass to you.

For an operator picking a processor, the questions that matter aren't about interface or price alone. Ask who their bank sponsors are, how many independent rails sit behind the terminal, and what their actual track record is when a card network has pulled support in the past. The companies that survive in this space aren't the ones with the slickest tech. They're the ones with the deepest bench of backup plans.
