SB3 was not really about THC. It was about who runs Texas.

The bill itself was simple and brutal: a near-total ban on THC products in the state, hemp-derived THC included. It passed the legislature. Then Governor Greg Abbott vetoed it. Jim Higdon, co-founder of Cornbread Hemp and the US Hemp Roundtable, laid out why on [Trump, Texas SB3 & the Rescheduling Timeline](/episodes/trump-texas-sb3-the-rescheduling-timeline-jim-higdon-on-what-comes-next): Trump pollster Tony Fabrizio ran the numbers and found the ban was an 80-20 loser statewide, unpopular even with Republican primary voters. A ban that unpopular doesn't survive contact with an election calendar.

## The veto was a power struggle, not a policy debate

Higdon frames this as a fight between Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick, who pushed the ban, and the governor's office, which killed it. Abbott didn't veto SB3 because he loves hemp THC. He vetoed it because Patrick tried to force through something the data said would hurt Republicans. That distinction matters for how operators should read this. It's not a durable win built on consumer protection or market logic. It's a veto that happened to land in the industry's favor this time.

## This is not over, it moves to a special session

Texas has a special session starting July 21 where this fight resumes. Nothing about the veto makes hemp THC permanently legal in Texas. It just means the total ban didn't survive its first attempt. Higdon's read: "Prohibition failed in Texas. It's a big deal." Bryan Fields put it plainly too: "The genie can't go back in the box." That's the operator takeaway. Hemp THC products are now a real constituency with real voters attached, and any future ban has to clear that same political math.

## The same fight is happening in Congress, with sharper teeth

What Texas tried to do with a blunt ban, Washington is trying to do with definitions. The Andy Harris amendment attached to a House appropriations bill would shift hemp's legal definition from a Delta-9 THC standard to a total THC standard, and ban any "quantifiable" amount of THC in hemp products. Higdon is direct about what that means: it would wipe out the THCA flower market entirely, since THCA converts to Delta-9 when heated, and it would cap delta-8 and HHC at 0.3%, closing the loophole that lets synthetic cannabinoids skip the Delta-9 threshold. The current 0.3% rule only exists because Congress in 2018 was thinking about CBD droppers, not multi-gram gummies or beverages. Nobody did the math on what 0.3% means in a heavier product.

## What it means for operators

Don't treat the SB3 veto as a settled outcome. Treat it as evidence that outright bans are politically expensive once a THC category has real consumer adoption, in Texas and potentially in Congress. The bigger threat to hemp THC isn't a headline ban, it's a quiet redefinition like the Harris amendment that accomplishes the same result through a total THC standard. Watch the special session and the appropriations fight together. Higdon expects real movement after the August recess, and any direct contact between Trump and the Texas governor's office is worth paying attention to as a signal.
