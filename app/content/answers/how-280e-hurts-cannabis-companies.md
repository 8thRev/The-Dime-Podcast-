280E exists because the IRS treats every state-licensed cannabis company as a drug trafficker. The rule denies normal business expense deductions, rent, marketing, salaries, to any company "trafficking" in a Schedule I or II substance. That means a dispensary pays federal tax on gross profit, not net income, while a liquor store next door deducts everything. Jon Purow of Greenspoon Marder called this the single biggest lever in the industry, bigger than SAFE Banking, because eliminating it could boost profitability by up to 30 percent industry-wide, as he explained in [Emergency NY Update, Unknown Impact of Cannabis Rescheduling & IP Protection ft. Jon Purow](/episodes/emergency-ny-update-unknown-impact-of-cannabis-rescheduling-ip-protection-ft-jon-purow).

## 280E is a tax problem, not an enforcement problem

A lot of operators conflate 280E with federal prosecution risk. They're not the same thing. Attorney Shane Pennington was blunt about this in [Emergency Update: Shane Pennington Breaks Down Cannabis Rescheduling](/episodes/emergency-update-shane-pennington-breaks-down-cannabis-rescheduling): schedule one already gives the federal government full authority to enforce against cannabis businesses, and it lacks the funding, staffing, and state cooperation to do it. 280E is a separate mechanism, tied specifically to the tax code's definition of trafficking in a schedule one or two substance. Moving cannabis to schedule three doesn't create new enforcement exposure. It removes the tax penalty, because the trafficking definition no longer applies.

## Rescheduling fixes 280E, it doesn't worsen it

There's a myth floating around that rescheduling to Schedule III could somehow make 280E exposure worse. Pennington's read is the opposite. 280E is written to apply only to schedule one and two substances. If cannabis moves to three, the standard 280E tax treatment stops applying to cannabis businesses, full stop, even though the business is still technically violating federal law in other ways. This is the whole reason rescheduling matters more to operators' bottom lines than most of the policy fights getting attention right now.

## Hemp competitors don't pay this tax at all

Here's the part that should really bother licensed operators. Hemp-derived psychoactive products, Delta-8, THC-O, and the rest, aren't schedule one or two substances, so 280E never touches them. Purow flagged this as one of the industry's biggest unresolved distortions: sellers can move nationally through convenience stores and mail order, skip state licensing costs, and skip 280E entirely, while a state-licensed dispensary down the street eats both. The 2023 Farm Bill was the last real shot Congress had to close that gap, and it didn't.

What this means day to day: your effective tax rate isn't a rounding error, it's a structural disadvantage against both illicit and hemp-channel competitors who never touch it. Until rescheduling actually lands, cost discipline and clean books matter more than in almost any other regulated industry, because the tax code is actively working against your margin every quarter you stay open.
