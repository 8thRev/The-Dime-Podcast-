Visa and Mastercard do not allow their networks to be used for direct cannabis purchases, full stop. That has not changed and nothing short of federal legalization looks likely to change it. Sundie Seefried of Safe Harbor Financial put it plainly on [Bank Secrecy, Hemp vs. Cannabis Banking, and Credit Card Barriers](/episodes/sundie-seefried-bank-secrecy-hemp-vs-cannabis-banking-and-credit-card-barriers-what-you-need-to-know-for-the-future): the major card brands have made clear they are not to be used for any cannabis product, anywhere in the country. Dispensaries can give a client a debit card to pay for dirt at Home Depot. They cannot run that same card through a terminal to pay for flower, at least not honestly.

## Misrepresenting the transaction is how workarounds survive, until they don't

Seefried described how some of these workarounds function: a broker sets up card machines, and the merchant gets categorized as something it is not, a flower shop in name only, not far off from a cultivation operation. That is lying to the financial system. Visa and Mastercard absorb risk they do not know they are absorbing. Once they figure it out, they blacklist it. Aubrey Amatelli of PayRio lived this directly. She told the story on [The Legal Market Was Never Built for Payments](/episodes/the-legal-market-was-never-built-for-payments-why-cannabis-still-breaks-the-rules-ft-aubrey-amatelli) of building a solution she believed was bulletproof, following every Visa and Mastercard rule, using the correct merchant category code, structured like a wallet technology. It still got shut down. Her conclusion: Visa and Mastercard are billion-dollar companies that can come in and squash whatever they want to squash, even if you can say you're doing it the right way.

## A shutdown freezes money, not just access

When a card brand pulls support, the processor's sponsoring bank is required to shut off that rail immediately, with no appeal first. Amatelli said funds already in flight can get frozen for up to 180 days, the standard window banks hold for potential chargebacks and fraud. For a cash-dependent operator, that is not a minor inconvenience. It is a liquidity event. Her answer to it was redundancy: stacking multiple processors, bank sponsors, and rails into one terminal, so if one gets cut off another activates without the dispensary noticing. She also built a crypto on-ramp, taking credit or debit cards and converting instantly into USDT or USDC for the merchant, though she noted usage sits under one percent of transactions.

## Rescheduling will not fix this, and operators mostly agree on that

Seefried argued descheduling would not deregulate cannabis banking, because Bank Secrecy Act obligations apply to any high-risk cash business regardless of a substance's federal schedule. Amatelli went further on the card side specifically: Visa and Mastercard require full federal legalization, not rescheduling, before they will treat cannabis like any other retail category. Jay Rosenthal, on [Who Benefits From Federal Cannabis Limbo?](/episodes/who-benefits-from-federal-cannabis-limbo-ft-jay-rosenthal), said credit card processing is one of the very few reforms almost everyone in the industry actually agrees on, because it would likely raise average basket sizes. That consensus exists precisely because nobody has solved it yet.

For an operator, the practical move is not to chase a permanent fix that doesn't exist yet. It is to pick a processor who is honest about the risk, builds in redundancy, and tells you plainly when to expect funds in flight rather than promising a workaround will never get caught.
